Yes, boredom is good for your child, and the urge to rescue them from it is the bit worth resisting. When a three, four or five year old has nothing laid on and no screen within reach, their brain has to go looking for something to do, and that search is where imagination, problem-solving and self-reliance get built. The boredom itself is not the prize. What your child does to climb out of it is.

The hard part is holding off long enough to let the boredom turn into something. Here is what a bit of boredom does for a young child, what the research shows, and how to hold your nerve when you hear the famous whine.

## Why boredom is not the enemy

Most of us treat an empty, grizzly ten minutes as a problem to be fixed, and the fastest fix is usually a screen. The trouble is that a child who is never left to fill a gap never learns that they can. Every time we step in with an activity or a tablet, we are doing the inventing for them, and the one muscle that matters here, the one that comes up with the idea, never gets a go.

Australia's government-funded [Raising Children Network](https://raisingchildren.net.au/preschoolers/videos/boredom-play-development) puts it plainly. When children "push through the boredom", they say, "that's when there's a huge amount of creative thinking and imaginative capacity and problem-solving that happens". The same guidance makes the case for deliberately not entertaining your child every waking minute, because working through a bit of boredom builds "a sense of being able to rely on themselves and resilience".

The empty moment is not wasted time. It is the run-up.

## What a bit of boredom builds

Boredom at this age is rarely just boredom. It is the pause before your child decides what to do next, and in that pause a few of the strengths that will matter most for the rest of their life get a workout:

- **Imagination and invention.** With nothing provided, a child starts making. A cardboard box becomes a boat, the washing basket becomes a cave, the backyard becomes a whole country with its own rules. This is the kind of self-directed, make-your-own-fun play that a filled-in day leaves no room for.
- **Problem-solving and planning.** Child psychologists at the [Child Mind Institute](https://childmind.org/article/the-benefits-of-boredom/) point out that when children build their own projects in unstructured time, they "have to create a plan, organise their materials, and solve problems". Those are big thinking skills, and they only switch on when nobody else is driving.
- **Agency and self-reliance.** Clinical psychologist Dr Stephanie Lee describes the goal as helping children "develop independence and feel agency over their own happiness". A child who learns they can turn a dull afternoon around is a child who is not waiting to be entertained. That is one of the [human strengths a screen can't teach](/blog/eight-human-skills-your-child-needs-in-an-ai-world/): being the one who decides and makes things happen.
- **Tolerating the flat feeling.** Boredom is mildly uncomfortable, and learning to sit with mild discomfort is its own skill. Dr Lee notes it helps children "build tolerance of less-than-ideal experiences", which is useful practice for a life that will not always be interesting on demand.

## What the research says about boredom and creativity

It is not only a nice idea. There is research to back it.

In a 2014 study published in the [Creativity Research Journal](https://doi.org/10.1080/10400419.2014.901073), psychologists Sandi Mann and Rebekah Cadman tested whether being bored first really does make people more creative. They had one group do a dull task before a creative one, and left a control group to go straight to the creative task. The bored group came up with more, and more inventive, ideas. The researchers suggested the daydreaming and mind-wandering that boredom sets off is what feeds the creativity that follows.

The idea goes back further. Teresa Belton, a researcher at the University of East Anglia who has studied boredom and children for years, argues that empty time is where imagination gets its room to move. Speaking to the [World Economic Forum](https://weforum.org/agenda/2016/09/being-bored-is-good-for-children-and-adults-this-is-why), she called it "very freeing, being creative for no other reason than that you freewheel and fill time". Her view, after interviewing creative adults about their childhoods, is that many of them trace their imagination straight back to being left alone with time on their hands.

None of this means a child should be bored all day. It means the odd stretch of nothing is doing more than it looks.

## What to do when you hear "I'm bored"

The instinct is to leap in with a suggestion or a screen. Try not to, at least not straight away. A few things that help:

- **Let it sit.** The first ten minutes of "I'm bored" are the uncomfortable bit, and they usually pass. Resist fixing it the second you hear it. Boredom that is allowed to keep going is boredom that turns into an idea.
- **Offer space and simple stuff, not a plan.** Belton's advice is to give children time, space and a few plain materials, then step back. A box of odds and ends, some paper and tape, a pile of blocks or a patch of garden beats a packaged activity, because the child has to supply the idea.
- **Do not swap a screen for a screen.** Handing over a tablet ends the boredom, but it also ends the thinking. It takes over the inventing your child was about to do. If you want more on where screens fit at this age, we go into it in [how much screen time is right for a 3 to 5 year old](/blog/how-much-screen-time-for-3-to-5-year-olds/).
- **Hold your nerve through the whinge.** "I'm bored" is often a test of whether you will produce the entertainment. When you calmly do not, your child learns the job is theirs, and most of the time they get on with it.

## Questions parents ask about boredom

**Is it OK to let my preschooler get bored?**
Yes. A bit of boredom is good for young children. It pushes them to invent their own play, solve their own small problems and learn they can rely on themselves, which is where creativity and independence grow. The key is letting it happen now and then, not leaving a child bored and ignored all day.

**How long should I let my child be bored before I step in?**
There is no set number, but give it a good ten minutes or so before offering anything. The first stretch of boredom is the uncomfortable part, and it usually passes into play on its own. Stepping in too fast does the inventing for them.

**What should I do when my child says "I'm bored"?**
Stay calm and do not rush to fix it. Offer time, space and a few simple open-ended materials rather than a packaged activity or a screen, then step back. Most children work their way into something once they realise the entertainment is not coming.

**Is boredom better than screen time for my child?**
In many moments, yes. A screen ends the boredom but also ends the imaginative, problem-solving thinking your child was about to do. Boredom hands that thinking back to them. Screens have their place, but reaching for one at the first grizzle means your child misses the best part.
