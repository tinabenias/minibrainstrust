The short answer is to go easy on "you're so clever" and reach instead for "you worked so hard at that." Both come from a good place, but they teach very different lessons. Praising the trait tells a child that success means being born smart, so when something gets hard they tend to back off to protect the label. Praising the effort tells them that trying is what gets results, so they stick with the hard thing a little longer. You are not banning the word clever. You are just aiming most of your praise at what your child did, not at what they supposedly are.

Here is what the research found, why it matters most between three and five, and the small change to what you say that makes the difference.

## The two kinds of praise

Almost all the praise we give lands in one of two buckets, and it helps to be able to tell them apart.

The first is **person praise**, aimed at the child themselves. "You're so clever." "What a smart girl." "You're a natural." It feels lovely to say and lovely to hear, but it points at a fixed quality, something the child either has or does not.

The second is **process praise**, aimed at what the child did. "You kept trying different ways until it worked." "You really concentrated on that." "You did not give up when it got tricky." It points at effort, strategy and choices, all things a child can do more of tomorrow.

Australia's government-funded [Raising Children Network](https://raisingchildren.net.au/toddlers/connecting-communicating/connecting/praise) draws the same line in plain terms. It calls out descriptive praise, where "you tell your child exactly what it is that they've done well," as more useful than a vague "you're a good boy," and it puts effort at the centre: "Encouragement is praise for effort," and praising effort "can motivate your child and encourage them to try hard in the future." When praise lands on effort, it says, "children are more likely to see trying hard as good in itself."

## Why "you're so clever" can backfire

This is not just a nice idea. One of the best-known studies in the field put it to the test, and the results surprised a lot of parents.

Across six experiments with just over four hundred schoolchildren, published in the [Journal of Personality and Social Psychology](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf), researchers Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck gave children a set of puzzles, let them succeed, and then praised them in one of two ways. Some were told they must be clever. Others were told they must have worked hard. That single sentence was the only difference.

Then the researchers made the next puzzles much harder, so every child hit some failure. What happened next split cleanly along the line of what they had been praised for:

- **The clever group gave up more easily.** After the hard puzzles, children praised for being clever enjoyed the task less and were less keen to keep going, while the children praised for effort stayed interested.
- **They performed worse.** Faced with difficulty, the clever group's scores dropped, while the effort group's scores climbed. Same children, same puzzles, different praise.
- **They were more likely to lie about it.** When given a chance to report their scores to another child, 38% of those praised for being clever overstated how well they had done, compared with around one in eight of the children praised for effort.

The children in these studies were older, around ten, so this is not a direct snapshot of a preschooler. But the mechanism is the one worth understanding. If being clever is the thing you are proud of, then a hard problem becomes a threat to that, and the safest move is to avoid it or fudge it. If effort is the thing, a hard problem is just a reason to try harder. The praise had told each child what the game was.

## It starts younger than you'd think

The fair question is whether any of this applies to a three or four year old, who is not sitting puzzles for a researcher. A separate study suggests the groundwork is laid much earlier, right in the years you are in now.

Researchers videotaped more than fifty children at home with their parents at one, two and three years old, and coded how the parents praised them. Then they came back five years later, when the children were seven and eight. As reported in [Child Development](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655123/), the toddlers whose parents had used more process praise, the effort and strategy kind, grew into children who took a more positive approach to challenges, came up with more ways to get past a setback, and believed they could get better at things by trying. Person praise did not predict this. Neither did the sheer amount of praise. It was the kind that mattered.

In other words, the habit you build in these ordinary years, the words that fall out of your mouth when your child finishes a puzzle or a drawing, is already shaping how they will meet hard things at school. That is a powerful thing to know on a Tuesday afternoon.

## What to say instead

You do not need a script, and you certainly do not need to catch every single thing you say. You just need to nudge your default a little, toward the effort and away from the trait. A few swaps that do the work:

- **Name the effort, not the brain.** Instead of "you're so smart," try "you kept going even when it was tricky." Instead of "good girl," try "you waited so patiently for your turn."
- **Be specific about what they did.** "You used lots of blue for the sky" tells a child more, and feels more genuine, than "that's beautiful." Children can tell the difference between real notice and a reflex.
- **Praise the strategy, not just the result.** "You tried the big piece first, that was clever thinking" celebrates how they got there, which is the part they can repeat next time.
- **Let plenty go by without comment.** Not everything needs a cheer. A warm face, an interested question ("how did you make it balance?") and simply watching are often worth more than another "good job."
- **Go gently on the praise that fixes a label.** "You're the best drawer in the family" sets up a title your child then has to defend. "You really stuck with that drawing" sets up nothing but more drawing.

None of this means being stingy with warmth. Praise your child often, much more than you correct them, as the Raising Children Network suggests. Just aim it, most of the time, at the trying.

## Questions parents ask about praising children

**Is it bad to tell my child they're smart?**
Not now and then, no. The odd "you're so clever" will not undo anything. The thing to avoid is making it your main message, because steady praise for being smart can teach a child that success is about a fixed quality, which makes them wary of anything hard that might prove otherwise. Aim most of your praise at effort and strategy, and the occasional "you're clever" sits harmlessly on top.

**What should I say instead of "good job"?**
Say what you noticed. "You concentrated really hard on that." "You kept trying until the tower stood up." "You shared the blocks without me asking." Specific, effort-focused praise tells your child exactly what they did well, which is both more motivating and more believable than a quick "good job."

**Does praising effort work even if my child does not succeed?**
Yes, and this is the whole point of it. You can praise the trying, the strategy and the sticking-with-it whether or not the thing worked out. "You gave that a really good go" is true even when the tower falls. It teaches a child that effort is worth something in itself, which is exactly what keeps them willing to try the next hard thing.

**Can too much praise be a problem?**
Over-the-top or constant praise can lose its meaning, and children can tell when it is not genuine. You do not need to applaud everything. Warm attention, an interested question and simply being there often count for more than another cheer, so save your words for the moments you truly notice.
