The recommended limit is lower than most parents expect. For a child aged two to five, health authorities say no more than one hour of screen time a day, and they are clear that less is better. That figure comes from both the [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241550536) and Australia's own [24-hour movement guidelines for the early years](https://www.health.gov.au/topics/physical-activity/24-hour-movement-guidelines-for-all-australians). For children under two, the advice is no screen time at all.

This is not a ban, and it is not here to make you feel bad about the iPad that got you through a long car trip. It is about protecting the hours when your child's brain is doing its fastest and most important building, and knowing why those hours matter makes the limit a lot easier to hold.

## What the official guidelines actually say

The WHO reviewed the evidence in 2019 and landed on a simple line for two to four year olds: sedentary screen time should be no more than one hour, and less is better. Australia's guidelines, which cover birth to five, say the same thing. No screens before two. From two to five, cap it around an hour a day and treat that as a ceiling rather than a target.

Worth noting what "screen time" means here. It is the passive, sitting-still kind: watching, scrolling, tapping through a game on your own. A video call with Grandma on the other side of the country is a different thing, and the guidelines treat it that way. It is real connection that happens to travel down a screen.

## Where the one-hour number comes from

The limit is not plucked from the air. One of the clearest pieces of evidence is a large Canadian study published in [JAMA Pediatrics](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2722666) in 2019, which followed 2,441 families and tracked both how much screen time children had and how they were developing across language, motor skills, problem solving and social skills.

The pattern was consistent. More screen time at ages two and three went with poorer development a year or two later. The researchers also did the thing good researchers do: they checked whether it might be the other way around, with slower-developing children simply being parked in front of screens more. It was not. The screen time came first, and the developmental lag followed.

One study never settles a question on its own, and this one shows a link rather than proving cause beyond doubt. But it is large, carefully run, and it points the same way as the wider body of evidence the guidelines are built on. When the pattern keeps repeating, it is worth taking seriously.

## It is less about the screen, more about what it replaces

The strongest case against too much screen time is not that a screen harms a child in some direct way. It is about what the screen quietly crowds out.

An hour in front of a device is an hour not spent doing the things a young brain is actually built for at this age: the back-and-forth chatter that grows language, the hands-on fiddling that wires up fine motor skills, the stretch of boredom that turns, if you leave it alone long enough, into invention. These are the [human strengths a screen can't teach](/blog/human-skills-ai-cant-replace/), and three to five is exactly when the ground is softest for growing them.

That is the lens we would gently suggest. Not "screens are evil," but "what is this hour costing us." Some days the answer is nothing much, and that is fine. Other days the answer is a whole afternoon of the good stuff, and that is worth noticing.

## What good screen time looks like when you do use it

Screens are part of modern life and you do not have to pretend otherwise. When you do reach for one, a few things make it far better:

- **Watch or play together where you can.** A child learns more from ten minutes you share and talk about than an hour they spend alone. Point things out, ask what they think, laugh at the silly bits.
- **Choose slow over frantic.** Gentle, well-made programmes beat the fast, flashing, auto-playing stuff that is designed to be hard to switch off.
- **Keep it out of the bedroom and off the dinner table.** Protecting sleep and mealtime talk does more good than policing the exact number of minutes.
- **Use it for connection.** Video calls with family who live far away are a genuinely lovely use of a screen.

## Screen time questions parents ask

**How much screen time should a 3 year old have?**
No more than one hour a day, and less is better, according to both the World Health Organization and Australia's movement guidelines. That hour is a ceiling for the passive, sitting-still kind of screen use, not a daily quota to fill.

**Is screen time bad for a 4 or 5 year old?**
It is more about balance than harm. The same one-hour guideline covers the whole two-to-five range. The concern is less about the screen itself and more about the play, talk and movement that screen time replaces, all of which matter enormously at this age.

**Does educational TV or a learning app count towards the limit?**
Yes, it still counts as screen time, though watching or playing together and talking about it is far better than your child using it alone. Quality and company help. They do not make the minutes disappear.

**How do I cut down on screen time without a meltdown?**
Give some warning before it ends, and offer a hands-on activity to move towards rather than just switching the screen off into nothing. Our [witching-hour resets](/blog/beating-the-witching-hour/) work well for tricky transitions, because a child copes with change far better when there is something inviting on the other side of it.

**Is it too late if we are already over an hour a day?**
Not at all. The guidelines are a direction, not a scorecard. Trimming back gradually and filling the gap with real play does your child good starting from whenever you begin.
