The quickest way to get two young children playing together is not to invent the perfect game or buy another toy. It is to give them one simple, open-ended thing to do side by side, a big cardboard box, a pile of cushions, a bowl of water and some cups, and then step back and let them work out the rest. Young children learn to play together the same way they learn everything else at this age, by doing it, badly at first, with you nearby to steady the wobbles rather than referee every second. The squabbling in between is not a sign it is going wrong. It is a big part of how they learn.

If it feels like your two fight more than they play, you are not imagining it and you are not doing anything wrong. In a review of sibling research, the psychologist Laurie Kramer notes that two to four year olds can have [almost eight disputes an hour](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.northeastern.edu/dist/d/3128/files/2023/02/Kramer-2010-Essential-Ingredients.pdf) when they are together. Eight. An hour. So if you spent this morning umpiring one row after another, that is not a broken sibling relationship. That is a completely ordinary one.

## The fighting is the practice, not the problem

It helps to see all that bickering for what it is. When two small people argue over who had the blue cup first, they are running live practice in some of the hardest skills a human ever learns: taking turns, sharing, reading what someone else wants, standing their ground, backing down, and making up afterwards. There is no worksheet for any of that. It is learned by bumping into another person who wants the same cup you do.

That is also why sibling conflict is treated as a normal part of growing up by Australia's [Raising Children Network](https://raisingchildren.net.au/preschoolers/behaviour/friends-siblings/why-children-fight), the government parenting service. The goal was never a house with no arguments. The goal is children who slowly get better at handling the arguments they have, and your two cannot practise that on a screen or alone in separate rooms. They can only practise it on each other.

Kramer's work makes one more point worth holding onto. Cutting out the fighting is not the same as building a good relationship. In her words, warm sibling play does not simply appear once the squabbling stops. The positive side has to be grown on purpose, which is the part we will get to.

## What playing together is really teaching them

Here is the lovely bit, the reason it is worth the effort on the hard days. When siblings play well together, especially the made-up, pretend kind of play, they are giving each other something no adult easily can.

Researchers at the University of Cambridge followed 140 children from the age of two for five years in a project called [Toddlers Up](https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/news/sibling-rivalry-and-brotherly-love). They found that a sibling is "a natural ally," a playmate on roughly the same wavelength, and that when siblings sink into pretend play together they end up talking about thoughts and feelings in a way that gives each other "emotional scaffolding." In plain English, working out who is the baddie and why the teddy is sad is how young children build their understanding of other people's minds.

The effect on the younger child is striking. At three, little siblings used far less talk about thoughts and feelings than their big brothers and sisters. By six, their social understanding had caught up fast, and they were discussing emotions about as often as their older siblings. Tagging along after an older sibling, even getting teased by one, turns out to be a rich early education in how people work. These are exactly the human strengths a screen cannot teach: reading a real face, sharing a real moment, sorting out a real disagreement.

## How to get them started (without hovering)

Most of the time, children play together better when we set the scene and then get out of the way. A few things that help:

- **Give them one shared mission, not a pile of toys.** "Build a house for the toy animals" or "make a boat we can both sit in" gives two children a reason to work as a team. A shared goal pulls them together far better than a room full of separate toys, which tends to pull them apart.
- **Reach for open-ended stuff over toys with one job.** A box, blocks, blankets, cushions, playdough and junk from the recycling can become anything, so there is room for both children's ideas. A toy that only does one thing usually ends up being fought over rather than shared.
- **Give each child a role.** The builder and the deliverer. The shopkeeper and the customer. The dragon and the knight. When everyone has a job, there is less standing around waiting for a turn, which is where most of the trouble starts.
- **Let the older one be the expert sometimes.** Little kids love showing a younger sibling how to do something, and younger ones love the attention. "Can you show your sister how to make the tower?" turns a rivalry into a team for a few minutes.
- **Resist the urge to improve it.** Their game will look chaotic and make no sense and that is fine. The moment you step in to make it fairer or better, it becomes your game, and they stop doing the hard work of running it themselves.

## When to step in, and when to stay out

The hardest skill here is often yours, not theirs: knowing when to leave a squabble alone. As a rough guide, the small stuff is theirs to sort out and the unsafe stuff is yours.

- **Stay close but stay out of the little rows.** If nobody is getting hurt, give them a minute to find their own way through before you swoop in. Every time you solve it for them, you take away a rep they needed.
- **Coach, do not judge.** Rushing in to find out "who started it" teaches children to compete for your verdict. It is more useful to name the problem and hand it back: "You both want the truck. What could we do so you each get a turn?"
- **Step in properly when it turns physical or cruel.** Hold the line calmly on the behaviour while staying even-handed. It is okay to be cross, it is not okay to hit. Try not to make the older one carry the blame every time just because they are bigger.
- **Help them make up, then let it go.** Young children move on fast. Once the storm passes, you do not need a long post-mortem. A quick "you sorted that out" and back to it is plenty.

## Playing together is a skill, not luck

It is easy to decide your two are just wired to clash, or that other families got the easy children. The research points somewhere more hopeful. Kramer went on to build parenting programs, including one called [More Fun With Sisters and Brothers](https://bouve.northeastern.edu/news/getting-along-with-your-siblings-can-be-taught-northeastern-research-shows/), on a simple finding: getting along with a sibling can be taught. The warmth, the turn-taking, the knack of playing as a team, these are not fixed traits you either lucked into or did not. They are skills, and skills grow with practice and a bit of coaching from you.

That reframes the whole job. You are not waiting and hoping your children will one day like each other. You are giving them small, regular chances to be on the same side, and nudging them along when they get stuck. Done often enough, those little moments add up to two kids who know how to play together.

## Questions parents ask about getting kids to play together

**Why do my kids fight so much?**
Because they are young, they are together a lot, and they are still learning every skill that stops fights: sharing, turn-taking, reading each other and backing down. Frequent squabbling between three to five year olds is normal, not a sign of a bad relationship. Research has recorded two to four year olds having close to eight disputes an hour, so a morning of bickering is ordinary, not a red flag.

**Should I let my kids sort out their own fights?**
For the small stuff, mostly yes. If nobody is getting hurt, give them a chance to find their own way through before you step in, because that is how they practise solving problems. Step in calmly when it turns physical or cruel, name the problem, and hand the solution back to them rather than deciding who was right.

**How do I get a 3 and a 5 year old to play together when they want different things?**
Give them one shared goal and a role each so the age gap becomes useful instead of annoying. The older one can be the builder or the shopkeeper while the younger one delivers or pays. Open-ended materials like boxes, blocks and dress-ups help too, because there is room for both children's ideas rather than one right way to play.

**Is it bad if my child would rather play alone?**
No. Solo play is good for young children and builds focus and imagination, so a child who likes their own company is doing something valuable, not something worrying. The aim is not for siblings to play together all day. It is for them to be able to, and to enjoy it some of the time.
