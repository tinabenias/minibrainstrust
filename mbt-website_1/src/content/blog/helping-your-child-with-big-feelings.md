When your three or four year old falls to pieces over the wrong colour cup, the fastest way to help is not to fix the cup or to talk them out of it. It is to stay calm yourself, get down to their level, and put simple words to what they are feeling. Something like, "you are so cross the cup is blue." You are lending them your calm and naming the storm, and for a brain this young, that is exactly the help it needs. The bigger skill of handling feelings alone comes later, and it comes from having done this with you, hundreds of times, first.

Here is the part that makes the hard afternoons easier to bear: a young child having big feelings is not being difficult or manipulative. The [Raising Children Network](https://raisingchildren.net.au/toddlers/behaviour/crying-tantrums/tantrums), Australia's government-funded parenting service, is clear that tantrums happen because young children are still at an early stage of emotional and language development and cannot yet manage or express what they feel. In other words, the meltdown is a sign of a brain that is still under construction, not a child who is giving you a hard time.

## Their brain has an accelerator but not much brake

Picture your child's emotional life as a car with a strong accelerator and brakes that are still being fitted. The feelings arrive fast and loud. The part of the brain that slows them down, thinks it through and chooses a calmer response is the last part to develop, and it keeps maturing well into the twenties.

So when we tell an upset four year old to calm down, we are asking them to use a brake they physically do not have yet. They are not refusing. They cannot. This is why "use your words" so often lands on deaf ears in the heat of it. The very system that would let them do that has gone offline for the moment.

Knowing this changes the goal. You are not trying to win the argument about the cup. You are trying to help their nervous system come back down, and then, over months and years, to help them slowly build a brake of their own.

## Borrow your calm to them first

Before any clever technique, there is one thing that does the heavy lifting, and it is you. Young children settle by borrowing the calm of the adult they trust. Researchers call this co-regulation, and it is the engine underneath everything else. Your steady voice, your unbothered face and your slow breathing tell their alarmed little brain that the danger has passed and it is safe to come down.

The Raising Children Network puts it plainly: if you can stay calm during a tantrum, [you give your child a model of calm behaviour](https://raisingchildren.net.au/toddlers/behaviour/crying-tantrums/tantrums). The reverse is also true. If you get loud and rattled, you pour petrol on the fire, because they are reading you for cues about how big this problem really is.

None of this means you have to feel calm. Most of us are quietly counting to ten with our jaw clenched. It means acting calm on the outside long enough for their storm to pass. A few things that help you hold the line:

- **Lower your voice instead of raising it.** Speak slower and quieter than feels natural. It pulls the volume of the whole moment down.
- **Get down to their height.** Looming over a small, overwhelmed person makes it worse. Kneeling makes you safe rather than scary.
- **Take one slow breath before you say anything.** It steadies you, and children copy breathing without being told to.

## Name it to tame it

Once you are calm, the single most useful thing you can do is give the feeling a name. "You are really angry." "That was so disappointing." "You wanted to keep playing and it is hard to stop."

This is not just being nice. There is brain science under it. In a well-known study from UCLA, researchers found that putting feelings into words [reduced activity in the amygdala](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x), the brain's alarm centre, while gently switching on the more thoughtful part of the brain. Labelling the emotion literally turns the volume down on it. Some clinicians sum it up as "name it to tame it," and it works on adults too, which is worth remembering next time you stub your toe.

For your child, naming feelings does two jobs at once. In the moment, it helps them settle. Over time, it hands them the vocabulary they will one day use to manage feelings on their own. A child who has heard "you are frustrated" a hundred times eventually learns to think it, and thinking it is the first step to handling it.

## What to skip, and why

A few of our instincts in the moment quietly make things harder.

- **Reaching for a screen.** It works for ten minutes and buys nothing. A device does not teach a child anything about their feelings. It only presses pause on them, and the storm often lands later, usually at bedtime.
- **Long explanations.** A brain in full meltdown cannot follow a reasoned case for why we share the trucks. Save the chat for afterwards, when they can actually hear you.
- **"You're okay, stop crying."** It is meant kindly, but it tells a child their feeling is wrong. Feelings are not the problem. What we do with them is. Let the feeling happen, and hold the line on the behaviour. It is okay to be angry, it is not okay to hit.
- **Rushing to fix it.** You do not have to make the sad thing un-happen. Often a child needs their feeling witnessed far more than they need it solved.

## This is a skill they are learning, not a stage to survive

It is tempting to grit your teeth and wait for your child to grow out of big feelings. But managing emotions is not something children simply age into. It is a skill they learn, mostly from doing it alongside you, again and again, on hundreds of ordinary bad afternoons.

And it is worth the effort. A landmark study that followed around a thousand people from birth into their thirties found that children with better self-control grew up to have [better health, more financial security and fewer run-ins with the law](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1010076108), right across the range, not only at the extremes. The ability to pause and manage a feeling turns out to be one of the quietest and most powerful advantages you can help a child build. Every time you sit with them through a storm instead of shutting it down, you are laying a brick in that wall.

## Big feelings questions parents ask

**Why does my 4 year old have such big emotional reactions?**
Because the feeling part of their brain is well ahead of the thinking part that calms it down. Strong reactions to small things are a normal sign of a still-developing brain, not bad behaviour or bad parenting. It genuinely gets easier as they mature and their language grows.

**Should I ignore my child's tantrum?**
Ignoring the child can leave them feeling alone with a feeling too big to handle. It is usually better to stay nearby, stay calm, and quietly acknowledge the feeling while holding firm on any limit. You are not rewarding the tantrum. You are helping their nervous system settle and showing them feelings are safe to have.

**How do I teach my child to calm down?**
Mostly by calming down with them, over and over, until they slowly learn to do it themselves. Name the feeling, keep your own voice low and steady, and once they are settled, you can practise simple tools together in calm moments, like taking slow breaths or naming feelings in a picture book. The skill is built in the quiet times and drawn on in the loud ones.

**Is it bad to give a screen to calm my child down?**
The odd time will not harm anyone, and every parent has done it. As a daily habit it tends to backfire, because a screen pauses feelings rather than teaching a child anything about handling them, and the meltdown often reappears later. A short, hands-on activity settles them and builds the skill at the same time.
