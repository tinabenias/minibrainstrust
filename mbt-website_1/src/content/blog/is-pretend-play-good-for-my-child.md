Yes, pretend play is good for your child, and it is doing more than it looks. When your three to five year old turns a cardboard box into a rocket or feeds a wooden spoon to a teddy, they are practising language, working out how other people think, and holding themselves to a made-up set of rules. It is one of the richest kinds of play at this age, and the best part is that you do not need to stage it. You need to leave room for it, and sometimes get out of the way.

Here is what pretend play actually is, what it looks like between three and five, what the research says it does, and how to make space for it without turning it into a lesson.

## What pretend play actually is

Pretend play, also called imaginative or make-believe play, is any play where your child treats one thing as another, or acts out a role that is not really happening. A banana becomes a phone. The couch becomes a pirate ship. Your child becomes the mum, the dog, the doctor or the baby.

It usually gets going in a big way around two to three and really takes off across the preschool years. By then it stops being simple (feeding a doll) and gets layered and plotted, with characters, rules and storylines that can run for days.

For a young child, pretend play is some of the hardest mental work they do, dressed up as fun.

## What it looks like at 3 to 5

Pretend play changes a lot across these three years, and knowing roughly what to expect takes the worry out of it.

Australia's government-funded [Raising Children Network](https://raisingchildren.net.au/preschoolers/play-learning/play-preschooler-development/imagining-play-preschoolers) describes the rough shape of it:

- **Around three**, children love dramatic play with puppets and dress-ups, and will tell you long, detailed stories about things that never happened. An imaginary friend is common and completely normal.
- **Around four**, they start drawing simple pictures of places, people and things straight out of their imagination.
- **Around five**, they use drawing and play to tell whole stories, and they still cannot always tell what is real and what is made up. That is part of the fun, not a problem to correct.

If your child is a bit ahead on one part and behind on another, that is ordinary. What you are looking for is pretend play turning up at all, and slowly getting richer over months.

## What the research says it does

Pretend play has been studied for decades, and the picture is more interesting than "it makes children smarter."

**It feeds language.** This is the strongest finding. A 2018 meta-analysis by researchers at the Australian National University, pulling together many separate studies, found a robust [link between symbolic (pretend) play and language development](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dr.2018.05.005), a small-to-medium association that held up across different ages and study designs. It makes sense when you watch it happen. Pretend play is full of talk: narrating, negotiating roles, trying out new words and giving voice to characters.

**It is a workout for self-control.** Pretend play has rules, even when children invent them on the spot. The child playing the sleeping baby has to lie still. The shopkeeper has to wait for a customer. Researchers revisiting [Vygotsky's work on play and self-regulation](https://www.museumofplay.org/app/uploads/2022/01/6-1-article-play-and-self-regulation.pdf) point out that play is the first activity where a young child acts against their immediate impulse, holding to the role rather than grabbing what they want. Vygotsky put it famously: in play, a child stands "a head taller than himself." In one classic study they describe, the gap between how much self-control children showed in a pretend game versus a plain task was widest at age five.

**It helps them work out other people.** When your child plays the grumpy customer or the worried parent, they are stepping into someone else's head and trying on how that person might feel and act. The Raising Children Network notes that this kind of play helps children express feelings, practise communication, and make decisions and solve problems in a safe, invented world.

**And a dose of honesty.** The biggest review of the evidence, [Lillard and colleagues in 2013](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0029321), looked hard at the claim that pretend play is the one crucial ingredient in healthy development and concluded the strong version does not hold up. Pretend play looks like one good route among several, not a magic switch. That is actually freeing. You do not have to engineer the perfect imaginative scenario or buy the right kit. You just have to protect the time and space for it, because it is one of the natural ways children build these skills, and it happens on its own when you let it.

Imagination is also one of the [human strengths a screen cannot build for your child](/blog/eight-human-skills-your-child-needs-in-an-ai-world/), and pretend play is where it grows.

## How to make room for it

Pretend play is the easiest kind to support, because the best thing you can often do is less. A few things that help:

- **Offer open-ended props, not finished toys.** A box, a few scarves, some pots and a wooden spoon beat a toy that does one thing when you press a button. The less a toy decides for your child, the more their imagination has to do.
- **Follow their lead.** If the tea party turns into a vet clinic, go with it. The plot belongs to them. Your job is to be handed the pretend cup, not to improve the story.
- **Join in, then fade out.** It helps to start a game alongside them, then quietly let them take over. You are a prop and an audience, not the director.
- **Leave unstructured time in the day.** Pretend play needs stretches of nothing-in-particular to unfold. A little [boredom is often what tips a child into their best play](/blog/is-boredom-good-for-my-child/), so you do not need to fill every gap.
- **Narrate instead of quizzing.** "Your dragon looks hungry" keeps the game alive. "What colour is the dragon?" turns it into a test and tends to end it.

## Questions parents ask about pretend play

**At what age does pretend play start?**
Simple pretend, like holding a toy phone to their ear, often appears around eighteen months to two years. It grows quickly from there, and by three to five it becomes layered, with roles, rules and storylines that can carry on for days.

**Is it normal for my child to have an imaginary friend?**
Yes. Imaginary friends are common and completely normal in the preschool years, and they are a sign of healthy imagination, not loneliness. There is usually nothing to worry about.

**Does pretend play really help with learning?**
It is strongly linked to language development and is a rich setting for building self-control and understanding other people. The research does not support the idea that it is the single crucial ingredient in development, but it is one of the natural ways children grow these skills, which is reason enough to protect it.

**My child plays pretend on their own a lot. Should I join in?**
Solo pretend play is valuable and worth leaving alone when your child is absorbed. Joining in now and then adds language and new ideas, as long as you follow their lead rather than take over the story.
