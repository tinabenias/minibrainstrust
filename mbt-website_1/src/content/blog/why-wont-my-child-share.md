When your three or four year old clutches a toy to their chest and howls "mine," it is easy to worry you are raising someone selfish. You almost certainly are not. Sharing is not a personality trait your child either has or lacks. It is a skill, and a surprisingly hard one, that develops slowly across early childhood. Most young children do not reliably share in a fair, give-and-take way until around age seven, so a 3 to 5 year old who struggles with it is right on track. What works at this age is not forcing them to hand things over, but patiently teaching turn-taking and praising the moments they get it right.

So if you feel like you are refereeing the same toy battle on repeat, you are not doing it wrong. You are watching a normal brain learn one of the harder human skills, one small negotiation at a time.

## Your child isn't being selfish, they're being three

Even very young children already know that sharing is the right thing to do. They just cannot make themselves do it yet, and that surprises most parents.

In a study published in the journal [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0059510), researchers Craig Smith, Peter Blake and Paul Harris tested children aged three to eight. The young children readily said that sharing equally was the fair thing to do, and even predicted that others would share equally with them. Then, given the chance to actually divide up stickers themselves, they kept the bigger share. The children did not reliably put their behaviour where their words were until around ages seven to eight. Three year olds, the researchers found, already knew the rule. Living up to it took another four years.

That gap between knowing and doing is the whole story of sharing at this age. Your child is not confused about what is fair. The part of them that can hold back a want, think of someone else and choose the harder, kinder option is simply still under construction.

## Real fairness arrives later than you'd think

If you have ever felt like your preschooler plays the long game only when it suits them, there is research for that too.

In a well-known study in the journal [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature07155), economist Ernst Fehr and colleagues played sharing games with 229 children aged three to eight. In one game, choosing the fair, equal option meant giving up a sweet of the child's own. At three and four, only about 9 percent of children chose to share fairly when it cost them something. By seven and eight, that had climbed to around 45 percent. The researchers found a real sense of fair play tends to kick in between the ages of six and seven, not before.

This matters because it resets the bar. A 3 to 5 year old who will not give up a toy they are enjoying is not failing a test most children of their age pass. Costly, give-something-up generosity is mostly a primary-school skill. Expecting it at three is a bit like expecting them to read a chapter book: they will get there, but pushing hard now mostly produces tears.

## Part of sharing is a skill you can't see

There is a lovely, hopeful finding tucked inside the research. A lot of the time, young children want to share. What they lack is the know-how to do it fairly.

Developmental scientists writing for [UC Berkeley's Greater Good Science Center](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/why_is_your_preschooler_not_sharing) describe exactly this. In their words, "it's not that they don't want to share. It's that they don't yet understand how to share fairly." When they looked at what actually predicted whether a child could divide things up evenly, the answer was not age or temperament. It was counting. Children who had a solid grasp of numbers were about twice as likely to share fairly as those who did not, because working out "one for you, one for me" quietly depends on being able to track amounts.

The reassuring takeaway is that sharing is not one big moral quality your child either has or does not have. It is a bundle of smaller skills: noticing how someone else feels, waiting, managing disappointment, and yes, even a bit of basic maths. Each one grows with time and practice. None of them grows faster because you insisted.

## Why forcing it usually backfires

When two children both want the one truck, the quickest-looking fix is to make the holder hand it over. It rarely teaches what we hope.

Forced sharing sends a confusing message. The child learns that if they make enough fuss, an adult will produce the thing they want, and the child who was happily playing learns that their turn can be snatched away at any moment by a grown-up. Neither child practises the actual skill, which is the patient back-and-forth of waiting, asking and taking turns.

Australia's [Sydney Children's Hospitals Network](https://www.schn.health.nsw.gov.au/kids-health-hub/growth-and-development/preschoolers/social-and-emotional-development) points parents toward turn-taking rather than confiscation. Practising turns at home, it notes, helps your child "build patience, understand the benefits of sharing and learn to negotiate." It also offers a gentle reality check for the heat of the moment: at this age, children "may struggle to see other people's point of view." They are not refusing to consider their friend's feelings. They often genuinely cannot picture them yet.

## What actually helps a young child learn to share

You cannot rush the development, but you can give it something to practise on every day. A few approaches do most of the work:

- **Teach turns, not instant sharing.** Instead of "give it to her," try "you can finish your turn, then it's Mia's turn." A child who trusts their turn will not be cut short is far more willing to pass something on. A simple timer can do the refereeing for you.
- **Narrate the other child's feelings.** Say what the other child might be feeling out loud: "Look at Noah's face, he really wants a go." You are lending your child the perspective-taking their brain cannot quite manage alone yet.
- **Catch and name the good moments.** When your child does share, notice it specifically: "You gave your brother the blue cup, that was so kind, look how happy he is." Praise for the exact behaviour makes it far more likely to happen again.
- **Let them keep a few things off-limits.** Before a playdate, help your child put away one or two most-treasured toys. Being generous is much easier when a child does not feel everything they love is up for grabs.
- **Model it in front of them.** Children learn sharing by watching the adults around them. "Would you like some of my toast?" and "Let's take turns picking the song" teach more than a lecture ever will.
- **Keep your expectations age-sized.** With a three year old, aim for short, supported turns, not spontaneous generosity. With a five year old, you can start to expect a bit more give and take. Match the ask to the child in front of you.

## Questions parents ask about sharing

**At what age should my child be able to share?**
Simple turn-taking can start from around three, with plenty of adult help, but fair sharing that costs the child something usually does not settle in until about seven. Research in Nature found a real sense of fair play tends to emerge between six and seven. A 3 to 5 year old who finds sharing hard is developing normally, not falling behind.

**Should I make my child share their toys?**
Forcing a child to hand over a toy they are using tends to teach the wrong lessons and skips the skill you actually want them to build. Turn-taking works better: let the child finish their turn, then help the toy move on. Australia's Sydney Children's Hospitals Network recommends practising taking turns and praising sharing rather than confiscating.

**Is it normal for my child to share one minute and refuse the next?**
Completely. Sharing depends on a stack of skills that are all still growing, including impulse control, reading another child's feelings and managing disappointment. On a rested, calm day those skills work better. When your child is tired, hungry or overwhelmed, they drop out first. The inconsistency is the developmental stage, not defiance.

**My child shares happily at kindy but not with their sibling. Why?**
Siblings get our least polished behaviour because home feels safe and the stakes feel higher. There is also more history and more competition for your attention. Spending a little one-on-one time with each child, and praising the good moments more than you police the bad ones, takes some of the heat out of it.
