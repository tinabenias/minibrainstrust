Yes, and it makes more difference than almost anything else you can do in ten minutes. Reading aloud to a three, four or five year old feeds them words they would never hear in ordinary chat, builds the attention and language a screen cannot, and gives their brain a gentle head start that shows up years later at school. You do not need to be a good reader, own many books, or finish the story. You need a lap, a few minutes, and a habit of coming back to it most days.

Here is what the research actually shows, and how to make one book do more, on the nights you have nothing left in the tank.

## The Australian research is unusually clear

Most parenting advice is a judgement call. Reading aloud is one of the few things where the evidence is strong and it points one way.

A large Australian study by economists Guyonne Kalb and Jan van Ours followed more than 4,000 children from the [Longitudinal Study of Australian Children](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2267171), tracking them from age four and five all the way to ten and eleven. They found that reading to a young child had a direct effect on their later reading and thinking skills, not just a link that vanished once you accounted for the family. In their words, the frequency of reading to children at a young age has a real causal effect on schooling outcomes, whatever the home background.

The size of the effect is what stands out. Reading to a four or five year old six to seven days a week, rather than twice a week or less, had the same effect on their reading skills as being almost a full year older. Reading three to five days a week was worth about six months. Those same children went on to score higher in both reading and numeracy on their Year 3 NAPLAN tests. A bedtime story, it turns out, quietly does the work of an extra year of growing up.

## The million words hiding in a bedtime story

The other well-known finding puts a number on why this happens. American researchers led by Jessica Logan worked out how many words a child hears through books alone, depending on how often they are read to. The paper, published in the [Journal of Developmental and Behavioral Pediatrics](https://journals.lww.com/jrnldbp/abstract/2019/06000/when_children_are_not_read_to_at_home__the_million.9.aspx), called the result the million word gap.

A child read one book a day hears around 290,000 more words by the time they turn five than a child who is not read to. A child read about five books a day hears roughly 1.4 million more. That is not a typo. Over a few years, a few minutes a night, the difference runs into the millions.

Those extra words matter because books are not written the way we talk. Picture books are full of language that rarely comes up over dinner: gloomy, enormous, whispered, brave. A child soaks up those words, and the shape of sentences too, long before they can read a single one themselves. Every story is quietly stocking the shelves they will reach for later.

## It is doing more than teaching words

If the numbers make it sound like homework, it is not. Reading aloud is one of the warmest, lowest effort things you will do all day, and the softer benefits matter just as much as the measurable ones.

Australia's government-funded [Raising Children Network](https://raisingchildren.net.au/preschoolers/play-learning/literacy-reading-stories/reading-storytelling) puts the everyday case plainly. Reading and storytelling promote your child's brain development and their ability to focus and concentrate, help them get to know sounds, words and language, and lay the foundation for early literacy. Just as importantly, the time you spend reading together builds your bond, because for a small child, being read to is being loved.

There is a quieter benefit too, and it matters more each year. Sitting still with a story, following it to the end, wondering what happens next, these are the muscles of attention. They are exactly the muscles a fast, tappable screen leaves unbuilt. Reading aloud grows the kind of slow, sustained focus that is getting harder to come by and more valuable because of it. It is one of [the human skills your child will need most in an AI world](/blog/eight-human-skills-your-child-needs-in-an-ai-world/).

## How you read matters as much as how often

The words on the page are only half of it. What lifts a book from nice to powerful is turning it into a back-and-forth rather than a performance.

Researchers call this dialogic reading, and [reviews of the evidence](https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/early-literacy-development/articles/dialogic-reading-effective-way-read-aloud-young-children) find that reading this way grows a young child's language faster than simply reading the words straight through. The idea is to hand the story back to your child instead of just delivering it. A few easy ways in:

- **Ask, do not just tell.** "What do you think he will do now?" or "Why is she sad?" turns a listener into a thinker.
- **Follow their finger.** If they point at the dog in the corner, stop and talk about the dog. Their curiosity is the lesson.
- **Add a little.** When they name the truck, you say "yes, a big red rubbish truck." You are stretching the sentence they gave you.
- **Let them tell it back.** Once a book is familiar, pause and let them fill in the line they know is coming.

None of this needs to be clever. A child who is asked questions and listened to learns far more than one who is simply read at.

## What this looks like on a normal, tired night

You do not have to do any of this perfectly, or even most nights. The research rewards the habit, not the performance.

- **Anytime is a good time.** It does not have to be bedtime. A book after breakfast or in the waiting room all counts.
- **The same book again is fine.** For the ninth night running is fine too. Repetition is how young children learn, not a sign of boredom.
- **You can stop early.** If they wander off halfway, you still both got something out of it. A short book read with warmth beats a long one read through gritted teeth.
- **Aim for one book a day.** Raising Children Network suggests trying to share at least one book daily. One is the target that matters, and everything past it is a bonus.
- **Let them see you read too.** Children copy what we do far more than what we tell them to do.

## Questions parents ask about reading aloud

**How long should I read to my 3 to 5 year old each day?**
There is no magic number, but aiming for at least one book a day is a good, realistic target. Even a few minutes counts, and reading most days matters far more than reading for a long time on one.

**My child will not sit still for a whole book. Am I doing it wrong?**
No. Wriggling, wandering off, and only wanting two pages are all normal at this age. Let them move, follow the bits they like, and keep sessions short. A book they enjoy for three minutes does more than one they endure for fifteen.

**Does it still help if I read the same book over and over?**
Very much. Repetition is one of the main ways young children learn language. Each reading lets them predict what comes next, notice new details, and take more of the words on board, so the tenth read is often doing more than the first.

**Is an audiobook or a reading app the same as me reading aloud?**
They have their place, but they are not a swap. A big part of the benefit comes from the back-and-forth with you, the questions, the pointing, the cuddle. A screen or a recording cannot read your child, and it is that responsiveness that grows their language most.
